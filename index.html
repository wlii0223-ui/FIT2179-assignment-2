How to read this story

Start with place The opening map shows where historical hazards have been recorded and which hazards are most visible across the country.
Separate frequency from impact A common hazard is not always the most damaging one, so the first section compares event counts with high-impact records.
Follow recovery demand DRFA activations shift the story from disaster occurrence to where formal recovery support was repeatedly needed.
End with people The final section connects geographic patterns to deaths, injuries and hospitalisations.

01. National footprint

Hazards cluster where climate, people and exposure overlap.

This section asks where natural hazards appear first. The map gives the spatial footprint, then the two companion charts show why the answer is not only about location. Floods appear most often, but the highest-impact records include cyclones, bushfires and heatwaves. In other words, frequency and severity describe different kinds of risk.

Interact: hazard filter, year slider and timeline brush all update the national map; hover points for event details.

Reading the footprint: records cluster around populated coastal corridors, and floods form the longest frequency row. The impact ranking prevents a misleading takeaway: the most frequent hazard is not automatically the most harmful event type. This sets up the next section, where the story moves from historical events to recent recovery demand.

02. Modern recovery burden

Flood and rainfall dominate formal recovery demand.

The question now changes from what happened? to where did recovery systems need to respond? DRFA activations are administrative records, so they do not count every disaster. They are still useful because they reveal repeated formal recovery demand. The main signal is clear: flood and rainfall dominate this recent recovery burden.

Modern recovery signal: DRFA activations are not a complete disaster catalogue; they show where formal support was activated. In the radial chart, longer spokes and larger circles mean larger recovery volumes. In the time chart, the largest recent peaks are driven mostly by flood / rainfall. This means the recovery story is not evenly distributed across hazards; it is strongly shaped by repeated water-related events.

03. Different places, different hazards

Eastern states carry the largest activation burden, but every state has its own hazard profile.

The national pattern hides important regional differences. The state map identifies where formal recovery demand accumulates, while the heatmap explains the mix behind those totals. New South Wales and Queensland stand out, but no state has exactly the same hazard profile. This is why the section combines geography, hazard type and time rather than using one ranking alone.

Read across each row to compare a state's hazard mix; read down each column to see where each hazard group concentrates.

State abbreviations: ACT = Australian Capital Territory; NSW = New South Wales; NT = Northern Territory; QLD = Queensland; SA = South Australia; TAS = Tasmania; VIC = Victoria; WA = Western Australia.

Regional pattern: New South Wales and Queensland carry the largest activation counts, but the heatmap shows that the same total can come from different hazard mixes. The small multiples then add timing: flood / rainfall peaks sharply in 2022, bushfire peaks earlier, and storm and cyclone recovery follow different rhythms. The takeaway is that disaster planning cannot rely on a single national average.

04. Repeated exposure

Some local government areas return to the recovery system again and again.

This section narrows the lens from states to local government areas. Repeated activations matter because they show communities that return to formal recovery systems again and again. Repeated activation is not a blame measure; it is evidence that geography, settlement and hazard recurrence can combine into persistent recovery pressure.

Repeated exposure: the leading LGAs are not presented as failures. They identify places where geography, settlement and hazard recurrence repeatedly intersect with recovery systems. In the link diagram, read left to right: DRFA category groups connect to hazard groups, and thicker curves indicate more activations. Together, the two charts explain why local recovery capacity matters even when the national story is dominated by one hazard type.

05. Human consequences

The human cost continues after the spectacular disaster event.

The final section brings the story back to people. Maps and recovery records show where pressure accumulates, but health records show who carries part of the burden. AIHW measures highlight hospitalisations and deaths, while the bubble chart identifies historical events where human impact was unusually concentrated. Recovery demand ultimately becomes a human burden, not only a geographic or administrative pattern.

Human endpoint: the final section shifts from places to people. The AIHW chart highlights subgroup burden, while the bubble chart shows that a few events produce disproportionate deaths or injuries. This is the reason the visualisation combines multiple idioms: risk should be read as frequency, geography, recovery demand and human outcome at the same time.

Conclusion

Disaster risk is a pattern, not a single event.

Across the evidence, floods appear most often, eastern states absorb the largest formal recovery burden, and several local government areas face repeated activations. The story is not that one hazard is always worst. Frequency, impact, recovery activation and health burden describe different dimensions of risk, and they only become meaningful when read together.

Data sources: NEMA DRFA Activation History by LGA; AIDR/AEMKH Disaster Events with Category Impact and Location; AIHW Forces of Nature.

Data note: DRFA activations indicate where formal disaster recovery assistance was activated; they do not represent every natural disaster event in Australia. Historical disaster records and AIHW health measures were cleaned and aggregated for comparison, so the visualisation should be read as an evidence-based overview rather than a complete disaster register.

Design framework: Munzer's What/Why/How framework guided the design. The data includes historical disaster events, DRFA recovery activations, state and LGA geography, and AIHW human impact measures; the task is to compare, locate and explain patterns of disaster exposure and recovery demand; the idioms include maps, bars, lines, stacked areas, heatmaps, unit grids, radial lollipops, chord-like links, lollipop plots, small multiples and tooltips.
