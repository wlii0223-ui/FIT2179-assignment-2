01. National footprint

Hazards cluster where climate, people and exposure overlap.

This opening asks where disaster records appear, which hazards are most common, and whether frequency is the same as impact. Use the controls to isolate a hazard, limit the latest year, or brush the timeline to update the map.

Interact: hazard filter, year slider and timeline brush all update the national map; hover points for event details.

Reading the footprint: the national map shows disaster records clustering around populated coastal corridors, while the frequency grid shows floods as the most common historical category. The high-impact ranking adds a second message: the most frequent hazard is not always the most severe, because a small number of cyclones, bushfires and heatwaves dominate the severe end of the record.

02. Modern recovery burden

Flood and rainfall dominate formal recovery demand.

This section shifts from recorded events to formal recovery demand. The question is not only what happened, but which hazards repeatedly triggered government recovery support.

Modern recovery signal: DRFA activations are not a complete disaster catalogue. They show where formal recovery support was activated, making them useful for identifying repeated public recovery demand after major events. In the radial chart, longer spokes and larger circles indicate larger activation volumes; flood and rainfall clearly dominate, so the time-series spike is mainly a story of repeated flood recovery.

03. Different places, different hazards

Eastern states carry the largest activation burden, but every state has its own hazard profile.

This section asks which states carry the largest recovery burden, and whether they face the same mix of hazards. The maps and profiles are designed for comparison rather than open-ended exploration.

Read across each row to compare a state's hazard mix; read down each column to see where each hazard group concentrates.

State abbreviations: ACT = Australian Capital Territory; NSW = New South Wales; NT = Northern Territory; QLD = Queensland; SA = South Australia; TAS = Tasmania; VIC = Victoria; WA = Western Australia.

Regional pattern: New South Wales and Queensland carry the largest activation counts, while the heatmap shows that each state combines hazards differently. The small multiples explain why one national trend is not enough: flood and rainfall peak sharply in 2022, bushfire peaks earlier, and storm and cyclone recovery follow different rhythms.

04. Repeated exposure

Some local government areas return to the recovery system again and again.

This section asks where recovery demand repeats at the local scale, and how DRFA categories connect to hazard groups. Repeated activation is not a blame measure; it signals places where hazard exposure, settlement and geography overlap.

Repeated exposure: the leading LGAs are not presented as failures. They identify places where geography, settlement and hazard recurrence repeatedly intersect with recovery systems. In the link diagram, the left side shows DRFA category groups and the right side shows hazard groups; thicker curves mean more activations, revealing how administrative recovery categories and hazard types combine.

05. Human consequences

The human cost continues after the spectacular disaster event.

The final question is who is affected after the event. AIHW injury reporting keeps the story grounded in people, while historical impact records show how a few extreme events can dominate the human consequence of natural hazards.

Human endpoint: the final section shifts from places to people. Hospitalisations and deaths show that natural hazards create measurable health burdens beyond the headline disaster event, so risk should be read as both a geographic pattern and a human outcome.

Conclusion

Disaster risk is a pattern, not a single event.

Across the evidence, floods appear most often, the eastern states absorb the largest formal recovery burden, and several local government areas face repeated activations. The story is not that one hazard is always worst. Frequency, impact, recovery activation and health burden describe different dimensions of risk, and they only become meaningful when read together.

Data sources: NEMA DRFA Activation History by LGA; AIDR/AEMKH Disaster Events with Category Impact and Location; AIHW Forces of Nature.

Data note: DRFA activations indicate where formal disaster recovery assistance was activated; they do not represent every natural disaster event in Australia. Historical disaster records and AIHW health measures were cleaned and aggregated for comparison, so the visualisation should be read as an evidence-based overview rather than a complete disaster register.

Design framework: Munzer's What/Why/How framework guided the design. The data includes historical disaster events, DRFA recovery activations, state and LGA geography, and AIHW human impact measures; the task is to compare, locate and explain patterns of disaster exposure and recovery demand; the idioms include maps, bars, lines, stacked areas, heatmaps, unit grids, radial lollipops, chord-like links, lollipop plots, small multiples and tooltips.
