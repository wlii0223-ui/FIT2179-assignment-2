01. National footprint

Hazards cluster where climate, people and exposure overlap.

Question: Where have major hazards appeared historically, and does frequent mean severe? Evidence: the map locates recorded events, while the companion charts separate event frequency from high-impact disasters. Takeaway: exposure is spatial, but severity is selective.

Interact: hazard filter, year slider and timeline brush all update the national map; hover points for event details.

Reading the footprint: records cluster around populated coastal corridors, and floods form the longest frequency row. But the impact ranking changes the interpretation: a smaller number of cyclones, bushfires and heatwaves dominate the severe end of the historical record.

02. Modern recovery burden

Flood and rainfall dominate formal recovery demand.

Question: Which hazards repeatedly trigger formal recovery support? Evidence: the yearly series shows when demand rose, and the radial chart compares the scale of each hazard group. Takeaway: recovery pressure is dominated by water.

Modern recovery signal: DRFA activations are not a complete disaster catalogue; they show where formal support was activated. In the radial chart, longer spokes and larger circles mean larger recovery volumes. Flood / rainfall dominates, so the national spike is mainly a story of repeated flood recovery rather than evenly distributed hazard pressure.

03. Different places, different hazards

Eastern states carry the largest activation burden, but every state has its own hazard profile.

Question: Do states carry the same kind of disaster burden? Evidence: the state map ranks total activations, while the heatmap and small multiples reveal different hazard mixes over time. Takeaway: the east carries more demand, but the hazard mix is not uniform.

Read across each row to compare a state's hazard mix; read down each column to see where each hazard group concentrates.

State abbreviations: ACT = Australian Capital Territory; NSW = New South Wales; NT = Northern Territory; QLD = Queensland; SA = South Australia; TAS = Tasmania; VIC = Victoria; WA = Western Australia.

Regional pattern: New South Wales and Queensland carry the largest activation counts. The heatmap shows that each state combines hazards differently, while the small multiples explain the timing: flood / rainfall peaks sharply in 2022, bushfire peaks earlier, and storm and cyclone recovery follow different rhythms.

04. Repeated exposure

Some local government areas return to the recovery system again and again.

Question: Which communities return to the recovery system again and again? Evidence: the lollipop plot ranks repeated LGA activations, and the link diagram shows how DRFA categories connect to hazard groups. Takeaway: repeated activation is a signal of recurring exposure.

Repeated exposure: the leading LGAs are not presented as failures. They identify places where geography, settlement and hazard recurrence repeatedly intersect with recovery systems. In the link diagram, read left to right: DRFA category groups connect to hazard groups, and thicker curves indicate more activations.

05. Human consequences

The human cost continues after the spectacular disaster event.

Question: What does disaster pressure mean for people? Evidence: AIHW injury reporting shows who appears in hospitalisation and death records, while the bubble chart identifies extreme historical human impacts. Takeaway: recovery demand ultimately becomes a human burden.

Human endpoint: the final section shifts from places to people. The AIHW chart highlights subgroup burden, while the bubble chart shows that a few events produce disproportionate deaths or injuries. Risk should therefore be read as both a geographic pattern and a human outcome.

Conclusion

Disaster risk is a pattern, not a single event.

Across the evidence, floods appear most often, eastern states absorb the largest formal recovery burden, and several local government areas face repeated activations. The story is not that one hazard is always worst. Frequency, impact, recovery activation and health burden describe different dimensions of risk, and they only become meaningful when read together.

Data sources: NEMA DRFA Activation History by LGA; AIDR/AEMKH Disaster Events with Category Impact and Location; AIHW Forces of Nature.

Data note: DRFA activations indicate where formal disaster recovery assistance was activated; they do not represent every natural disaster event in Australia. Historical disaster records and AIHW health measures were cleaned and aggregated for comparison, so the visualisation should be read as an evidence-based overview rather than a complete disaster register.

Design framework: Munzer's What/Why/How framework guided the design. The data includes historical disaster events, DRFA recovery activations, state and LGA geography, and AIHW human impact measures; the task is to compare, locate and explain patterns of disaster exposure and recovery demand; the idioms include maps, bars, lines, stacked areas, heatmaps, unit grids, radial lollipops, chord-like links, lollipop plots, small multiples and tooltips.
