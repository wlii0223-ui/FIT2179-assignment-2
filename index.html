01. National footprint

Hazards cluster where climate, people and exposure overlap.

Historical records show recurring flood, bushfire, storm, cyclone and heat events across Australia. Use the controls to isolate a hazard, limit the latest year, or brush the timeline to update the map.

Interact: hazard filter, year slider and timeline brush all update the national map; hover points for event details.

Reading the footprint: floods are the most frequent historical category, but the highest-impact records are not simply the most common hazards. A small number of cyclones, bushfires and heatwaves dominate the severe end of the historical record.

02. Modern recovery burden

Flood and rainfall dominate formal recovery demand.

The DRFA activation dataset records where disaster recovery assistance was activated. It does not measure every disaster directly, but it gives a strong view of repeated recovery demand.

Modern recovery signal: DRFA activations are not a complete disaster catalogue. They show where formal recovery support was activated, making them useful for identifying repeated public recovery demand after major events.

03. Different places, different hazards

Eastern states carry the largest activation burden, but every state has its own hazard profile.

Flood and rainfall appear across the country, while bushfire, storm and cyclone activations create different regional signatures. This section is designed for comparison rather than exploration.

State abbreviations: ACT = Australian Capital Territory; NSW = New South Wales; NT = Northern Territory; QLD = Queensland; SA = South Australia; TAS = Tasmania; VIC = Victoria; WA = Western Australia.

Regional pattern: New South Wales and Queensland carry the largest activation counts, while the heatmap shows that each state combines hazards differently. The comparison matters more than any single ranking.

04. Repeated exposure

Some local government areas return to the recovery system again and again.

Repeated activation is not a blame measure. It indicates communities where disaster recovery need has recurred, often because hazard exposure, settlement patterns and geography overlap.

Repeated exposure: the leading LGAs are not presented as failures. They identify places where geography, settlement and hazard recurrence repeatedly intersect with recovery systems.

05. Human consequences

The human cost continues after the spectacular disaster event.

AIHW injury reporting shows that forces of nature continue to cause hospitalisations and deaths. Historical impact records provide context for high-impact events, while recent health data keeps the final section focused on people.

Human endpoint: the final section shifts from places to people. Hospitalisations and deaths show that natural hazards continue to create measurable health burdens beyond the headline disaster event.

Conclusion

Disaster risk is a pattern, not a single event.

Across the evidence, floods appear most often, the eastern states absorb the largest formal recovery burden, and several local government areas face repeated activations. The pattern suggests that natural disaster impact is shaped by hazard exposure, settlement geography and the repeated need for recovery support.
